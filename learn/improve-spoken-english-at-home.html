How to improve spoken English at home: a 30-day plan

Spoken English · 9 min read · Updated October 2026

You don't need a classroom or a native-speaker friend to become fluent. You need a daily routine that forces you to produce English out loud — and a way to measure whether it's working.

Why reading and listening aren't enough

Most learners in India have strong passive English: they understand films, read newspapers and write decent emails. The problem appears when they have to speak. Speaking is a separate skill because it happens in real time. You have to find words, build a sentence and pronounce it while the other person waits. That only improves with repetition under mild pressure — exactly what a home routine can provide.

Think of it like cricket. Watching matches teaches you the rules; only net practice teaches you to bat. Your “nets” are ten to twenty minutes a day of speaking out loud.

The five daily habits

1. Shadowing (5 minutes)

Play a short clip of clear English — a TED talk, a news segment or a Conversation Lab line — and speak along with it, half a second behind. Copy the rhythm, the pauses and where the voice goes up and down. Shadowing trains your mouth and ear together and is the fastest way to sound more natural.

2. Self-talk (throughout the day)

Narrate what you're doing in English, quietly: “I'm making tea. I'll add less sugar today. Then I need to call the bank.” It feels strange for a week, then it becomes automatic. Self-talk is how you stop translating from Hindi in your head.

3. One-minute speeches (5 minutes)

Use the Just-a-minute tool to get a random topic and speak until the timer ends. Don't stop to correct yourself. The goal is flow, not perfection.

4. Record and review (5 minutes)

Record one answer on your phone every day. Listen back once. Note one thing to fix — a word you mispronounced, a filler you repeated, a sentence that went on too long. Our pace meter will count your words per minute and fillers for you.

5. Learn phrases, not words (5 minutes)

Fluent speakers think in chunks: “Let me get back to you on that”, “That makes sense”, “Could you walk me through it?” Learn five useful phrases a day from the phrasebook and use each one in a sentence of your own.

How to measure progress

Feelings are unreliable — some days you'll feel worse even while improving. Track three numbers instead:

  • Speaking pace: aim for 120–160 words per minute in conversation.
  • Fillers per 100 words: under 2 sounds confident.
  • Longest unbroken answer: how long you can speak on a topic without stopping. Moving from 30 seconds to 2 minutes is a major milestone.

Mistakes that slow people down

  • Studying grammar instead of speaking. Grammar helps accuracy, but fluency comes from use.
  • Waiting to be perfect. Native speakers make mistakes constantly. Clarity matters more than perfection.
  • Copying an accent instead of clarity. You don't need a British or American accent. You need to be easy to understand.
  • Only practising when motivated. Attach practice to something you already do — your morning tea or your commute.
