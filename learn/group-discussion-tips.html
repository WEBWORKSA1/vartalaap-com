Group discussion tips: how to stand out without shouting

Interviews · 7 min read · Updated October 2026

In a group discussion, the loudest person rarely wins. Evaluators are watching for something specific: can you think clearly, communicate under pressure and move a group forward?

What evaluators actually score

AreaWhat they look for
ContentRelevant points, facts, examples, both sides considered.
CommunicationClear, concise sentences; audible voice; good pace.
LeadershipGiving direction, bringing others in, summarising.
ListeningBuilding on others' points instead of repeating your own.
BehaviourCalm, respectful, no interrupting or personal attacks.

The four moves that win a GD

1. Open with structure

If you start, define the topic and suggest a direction: “Before we take sides, let's agree on what we mean by ‘gig work’ — and then look at it from the worker's, the company's and the government's angle.” This earns leadership points immediately.

2. Add value, don't add volume

Every time you speak, bring something new: a statistic, an example, a counter-point or a question. Repeating someone else's point in different words scores nothing.

3. Disagree with the idea, not the person

“I see why you'd say that, but the data on rural internet access suggests otherwise.” Calm disagreement shows confidence. Aggression shows the opposite.

4. Summarise at the end

If time allows, offer to summarise: “The group agrees that X, is divided on Y, and the open question is Z.” The summariser is often remembered as the leader.

Phrases you can use tomorrow

  • Entering: “If I could add one point here…”
  • Building: “Taking Priya's point further…”
  • Data: “One number that matters here is…”
  • Disagreeing: “I'd look at it slightly differently…”
  • Including: “Arjun, we haven't heard from you — what do you think?”
  • Redirecting: “We seem to be going in circles — can we look at the cost side?”
  • Closing: “To sum up what we've discussed…”

Common GD mistakes

  • Speaking first without a point, just to be first.
  • Reading memorised facts that don't fit the flow.
  • Talking only to the evaluator instead of the group.
  • Staying silent for the first ten minutes, then panicking.
  • Using fillers and long run-on sentences — keep each contribution to 20–40 seconds.

A one-week practice plan

Each day, use our GD topic generator. Spend one minute noting three points (one for, one against, one data point or example). Speak for two minutes, recording yourself. Check fillers with the pace meter. By day seven you'll have prepared seven topics and, more importantly, trained yourself to structure thoughts in under a minute.
